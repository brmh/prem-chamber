Court marriage is one of those subjects where almost everything people are told is slightly wrong. The procedure itself is not complicated. What causes trouble is the timeline, and the false promises made about shortening it.

## What a court marriage actually is

A marriage solemnised under the **Special Marriage Act, 1954**, before the Marriage Officer of the district. It does not require either person to convert or to follow any religious ceremony. It is available to any two people who meet the conditions in the Act, whatever their faith.

This is different from registering a marriage that has already happened. If your wedding took place at home or in a temple and you now need a certificate, that is [marriage registration](/guides/marriage-registration-up), a different and much quicker process.

## The conditions

At the time of the marriage:

- neither party has a spouse living;
- neither is incapable of giving valid consent, or suffers from a mental disorder of the kind described in the Act;
- the man has completed 21 years and the woman 18 years;
- the parties are not within the degrees of prohibited relationship set out in the Act.

## The sequence

1. **Notice of intended marriage.** Both parties sign a notice before the Marriage Officer of a district where at least one of them has resided for thirty days or more immediately before the notice.

2. **Entry and publication.** The notice is entered in the Marriage Notice Book and a copy is published at the office, where it remains open to inspection.

3. **Thirty days.** During this period any person may object, but only on the ground that the marriage would contravene one of the conditions above.

4. **Objection, if any.** The Marriage Officer inquires into an objection and decides it. An objection not founded on a ground in the Act does not stand.

5. **Declaration.** After thirty days, if no objection has succeeded, the parties and three witnesses sign a declaration before the Marriage Officer.

6. **Solemnisation and certificate.** The marriage is solemnised at the office, or at a reasonable distance from it on payment of the additional fee. The certificate is signed by the parties and the three witnesses, and entered in the Marriage Certificate Book. That entry is conclusive evidence of the marriage.

## What you will need

- Proof of date of birth for both, such as a school leaving certificate, passport or birth certificate
- Proof of residence for both, and specifically proof that one of you has lived in the district for thirty days
- Passport-size photographs of both
- Identity proof and photographs of three witnesses
- Affidavits as to marital status, and that the parties are not within a prohibited degree of relationship
- Decree of divorce or death certificate of a former spouse, where either party was married before

## The notice period, and why it cannot be shortened

This is the single most common question, and the answer is that the thirty days is statutory. No fee, no contact and no office can compress it.

Two things are sold as shortcuts and neither is a marriage:

- An **affidavit of marriage** attested by a notary. It is a sworn statement, nothing more. It does not solemnise a marriage and does not give you a marriage certificate.
- A **temple or arya samaj ceremony followed by registration**. This is genuine, but it is a marriage under personal law, not a court marriage, and it is available only where both parties are of the same faith or the relevant law otherwise applies. Where it applies, it is faster: the ceremony happens, and the marriage is then registered.

If speed matters more than the route, that second option is worth discussing. If the Special Marriage Act is the right or only route, thirty days is the floor.

## If there is a safety concern

Where a couple has reason to fear interference, that should be raised at the start rather than discovered halfway through. The publication of the notice is a real feature of the process and it is public. There are protective steps available, and the time to consider them is before the notice is filed, not after.

## Plan for about five to six weeks

Counting the preparation of documents and affidavits before the notice, the thirty days itself, and the appointment for solemnisation afterwards, most couples should expect five to six weeks end to end. Booking leave, travel or a reception before the notice is filed is a mistake worth avoiding.
