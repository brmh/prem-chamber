Almost every property dispute that reaches a chamber began with a purchase where nobody read the papers properly. The seller seemed genuine, the price seemed fair, and the broker was in a hurry.

A title check is not a formality. It is the only step in the whole transaction that can stop you losing everything you are about to pay.

## What is actually examined

**The chain of deeds.** Each transfer is read backwards: how did this property reach the seller, and how did it reach the person before them? Every link has to be complete and registered. A break in the chain does not heal with time.

**Encumbrance.** Whether the property is mortgaged, or has any charge on it. A property standing as security for someone's loan can be sold to you while the bank still holds its claim.

**The revenue or authority record.** Whether the khatauni or the authority's record actually shows the seller as the recorded owner, and whether mutation was ever done after the previous transfer. A seller whose own name never went into the record is a seller with a problem.

**Dues.** Development authority, society and municipal dues follow the property. They become yours on the day you buy.

**Litigation and attachment.** Whether there is a pending case, an attachment, an injunction or a stay affecting the property.

**Identity.** That the person selling is who they claim to be, and that where there are multiple owners, all of them are selling.

## The warning signs

Some things should slow a transaction down immediately.

**Sale offered only on a general power of attorney.** A GPA does not transfer ownership. If a sale deed cannot be executed, there is a reason, and it is usually the reason you should walk away.

**The seller will not give the earlier deeds.** "The old papers are with my brother" is the beginning of a long story. The chain is the title.

**Price well below the going rate for the colony.** Sometimes it is a genuine distress sale. Often it is a discount for a defect.

**Property inherited, with other heirs not party to the sale.** A share that has not been relinquished or partitioned is a claim waiting to appear.

**Possession with someone other than the seller.** A tenant, a caretaker or a relative in occupation is a separate problem that does not end at registration.

**Recent mutation with no supporting document.** Entries do get made. They do not create title on their own.

## The written opinion

At the end of a proper title check there should be something in writing. Not a verbal assurance that everything looks fine, but a short note setting out what was examined, what is clean, what is missing, and what must be obtained before any payment.

That note is useful twice: once when you decide whether to proceed, and again years later, when you are the seller and someone else's advocate is asking your side the same questions.

## Do it before the token, not after

The usual sequence is the wrong way round: token paid, then papers sent for checking. By then the negotiating position is gone and the pressure to proceed is real.

Send whatever papers exist first. Even photographs on WhatsApp are enough for a first reading, and most problems show up in the last two documents of the chain. The cost of this step is a fraction of one per cent of the purchase price, and it is the only part of the transaction that protects the rest of it.
