Most rent agreements in circulation are the same two pages, retyped by a broker with the names changed. They work until they are needed, which is the only time an agreement matters.

## The eleven-month convention

A lease of immovable property from year to year, or for any term exceeding one year, must be registered. Registration means stamp duty at the higher slab, a visit to the Sub-Registrar office, and both parties attending.

Eleven months sits just below that line. Hence the convention. It is a matter of convenience, not a rule that agreements must be for eleven months.

For a valuable commercial property, or where a tenant is investing in fit-outs, a registered lease for a longer term is often worth the extra cost to both sides, because it gives certainty that an eleven-month paper does not.

## What the agreement should actually contain

**The parties and the property.** Full names, addresses, and a precise description of what is being let, including which portions and which fixtures.

**Rent and due date.** Amount, the date it falls due, and the mode of payment. Bank transfer leaves a record; cash does not.

**Security deposit.** The amount, what it may be adjusted against, and when it is to be refunded. Most disputes at the end of a tenancy are about the deposit.

**Escalation.** Whether the rent increases, by how much and when. Without this clause the rent cannot be raised during the term.

**Term, lock-in and notice.** How long, whether either side is locked in for a minimum period, and how much notice is required to end it.

**Maintenance and repairs.** Who pays for what. The usual split is that structural repairs are the landlord's and day-to-day maintenance is the tenant's, but say it explicitly.

**Utilities.** Electricity, water, maintenance charges and property tax. Say who pays each, and what happens to arrears.

**Use and subletting.** What the premises may be used for, and whether the tenant may sublet or allow anyone else in.

**Inspection.** The landlord's right to inspect, on reasonable notice. Without this, entering the premises is a problem.

**Condition on handover.** What state the property is to be returned in, and what counts as fair wear and tear. An inventory and photographs at the start save an argument at the end.

## What a landlord must not do

When the rent stops coming, the temptation is to cut the electricity, change the locks, or move belongings out. All three are the wrong move. They do not get the property back, and they turn a straightforward recovery into a case where the tenant has a grievance of their own.

The route is a proper notice, then proceedings for eviction and arrears. At that stage, what the agreement says about default and notice does a great deal of work, which is exactly why the two-page broker format is a false economy.

## For tenants

A tenant should be equally careful about the things that bite later: that the person letting the property actually owns it, that the deposit terms are written down, that maintenance responsibilities are clear, and that the condition of the property at handover is recorded. Ask for a copy of the ownership document. A landlord with nothing to hide will not mind.
