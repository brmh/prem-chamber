A registry looks like a single event: everyone meets at the Sub-Registrar office, signs, and it is done. In practice it is a sequence, and almost everything that goes wrong goes wrong before the day of registration, not on it.

## 1. The seller's papers are read first

Before a rupee moves, the chain of title is read backwards. The question is not whether the seller has a deed. It is how the property came to the seller, and how it came to the person before them.

What is being looked for:

- whether each transfer in the chain is complete and registered;
- whether the property is mortgaged or has any charge on it;
- whether other heirs have an unsettled share, which is common in family property;
- whether authority or society dues are outstanding;
- whether there is a case, attachment or stay over the property.

This is the step buyers skip when they are in a hurry, and it is the only step that can prevent a total loss.

## 2. The value is worked out

Stamp duty is charged on the higher of the circle rate value and the consideration. So the circle rate for that exact locality and category is applied to the area, and compared with the agreed price. The higher figure governs.

Get this settled before the deed is drafted, not after. It affects the budget, and sometimes it affects the negotiation itself.

## 3. The deed is drafted

A sale deed is not a form to be filled. The parts that matter:

- **Description and schedule.** The property has to be identified so precisely that it cannot be confused with the plot next to it. Boundaries, measurements, plot or unit number, and the larger scheme it sits in.
- **Payment.** How much has been paid, when, and by what mode. Cheque and transfer details go into the deed.
- **Possession.** When possession passes. Handing over possession before the balance is paid is a common and avoidable mistake.
- **Covenants.** What the seller is promising about title, encumbrances and dues.
- **Indemnity.** What happens if one of those promises turns out to be untrue.

Both sides should read it before it is printed on stamp paper, because changing it afterwards means doing it again.

## 4. E-stamp and registration fee

The e-stamp certificate is generated in the name of the person liable to pay, normally the buyer, and the registration fee is paid. Check the spelling of names against the identity documents at this stage. A correction later is a separate and tedious exercise.

## 5. The slot is booked

The details are entered on the state registration portal and an appointment at the Sub-Registrar office is taken. The slot is a window rather than an exact minute.

## 6. The day of registration

Everyone attends: both parties and two witnesses, each with their own identity proof.

At the office, the documents are checked, photographs and thumb impressions are taken, and the deed is admitted before the Sub-Registrar and endorsed. That endorsement is the point at which the registration is complete.

The original is returned after scanning, sometimes the same day and sometimes after a few days, against the receipt. Keep the receipt.

## 7. Mutation, which is not optional in practice

Registration transfers ownership. It does not change the revenue or authority record. Until mutation is applied for and done, the record still carries the previous owner's name.

People discover this years later, at the worst possible moment: when they try to sell, when a bank asks for the record, or when a connection has to be transferred. Apply for mutation within a few weeks of the registry, and keep the order with the deed.

## The mistakes that actually cost money

**Paying most of the price against an unregistered agreement.** An agreement to sell is a promise. If the seller changes their mind, or sells to someone else, or dies, you are left holding a claim rather than a property.

**Buying on a general power of attorney.** A GPA does not convey title. The Supreme Court has said so plainly. If a sale deed is not possible, there is a reason, and the reason is usually the problem you are about to inherit.

**Taking possession before the balance is paid, or paying the balance before possession.** Both sides tend to want the other to go first. The deed should say exactly what happens when.

**Not checking the seller's identity properly.** Impersonation cases in property are not rare. Identity documents should be seen in original and matched with the papers in the chain.

**Skipping the no-dues certificate.** Authority, society and municipal dues follow the property, not the person who ran them up.

## What to have ready

- Previous sale deed or allotment letter of the seller, and the earlier deeds in the chain
- Latest khatauni or property tax receipt
- Identity and address proof of buyer, seller and two witnesses
- PAN, or Form 60 where PAN is not available
- Photographs of both parties
- No-dues certificate from the authority or society, where it applies
- Approved map, where the property is a constructed unit
