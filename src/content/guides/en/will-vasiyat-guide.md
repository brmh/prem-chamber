A will is a simple document that people put off for years and then make in a hurry. The result is a page written at home, signed in front of whoever was around, and discovered later by a family that reads it differently from how it was meant.

The law does not ask for much. What it asks for is worth getting right.

## What the law requires

- The testator must be of sound mind and not a minor.
- The will must be signed by the testator, or by someone else in their presence and by their direction.
- Two or more witnesses must attest it, each having seen the testator sign, and each signing in the testator's presence.

That is the whole legal requirement. No stamp paper, no registration, no lawyer is strictly necessary. Wills fail not because these requirements are hard but because of what is left out.

## What makes a will hard to challenge

**Witnesses who take nothing.** A witness who is also a beneficiary, or married to one, invites the argument that the will was procured. Choose two people with no interest in it.

**An explanation for unequal shares.** This is the single most useful thing a will can contain. If one child gets the house and another gets less, say why in the will itself — that the second was helped with a business, or already received a property, or whatever the truth is. An unexplained inequality is the standard ground of challenge.

**Precise description of each asset.** "My house in Ghaziabad" is a problem if there are two. Full description, plot or unit number, and the document under which it was acquired.

**A residuary clause.** What happens to anything not specifically mentioned, including assets acquired after the will was made.

**A substitution clause.** What happens if a beneficiary does not survive the testator.

**An executor.** Someone named to carry it out. Prefer someone local and practical over someone senior and distant.

**Evidence of capacity, where age or illness could be raised.** Where the testator is elderly or unwell, a doctor's certificate of sound mind on the date of execution closes off the most common line of attack.

## Registration

Registration is optional. An unregistered will is fully valid.

What registration does is put the date and the fact of execution on official record, before the Sub-Registrar, with the testator and both witnesses present. That makes the two usual allegations — that the document is a forgery, or that it was signed on some other date — much harder to run.

There is no stamp duty on a will. Registration involves only the registration fee.

## Changing it later

A will speaks from the date of death, not the date of signing, and can be changed any number of times while the testator is of sound mind.

- A **later will** revokes the earlier one. Say so expressly in the new one.
- A **codicil** makes a small change without rewriting the whole thing.
- Destroying the will with the intention of revoking it also revokes it.

If the earlier will was registered, register the new one too. A registered will followed by an unregistered later will is a dispute waiting to happen.

## Where to keep it

An unfound will is the same as no will. Tell the executor where it is. Keep the original in one safe place, and give a copy to the executor. If it is registered, the registration particulars make it traceable even if the original is lost.

## Gift now, or will later?

People often ask whether to transfer the property during their lifetime instead. The difference is control.

A **gift** takes effect immediately and is not easily taken back. A **will** takes effect only on death and can be changed as often as circumstances change.

Where the concern is who inherits, either can work. Where the concern is being looked after in old age, the difference between the two matters far more than the stamp duty saved.
